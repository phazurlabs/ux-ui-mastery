# Inspiration & Reference Guide — Curated Sources for Screen & Flow Design

> How to find, filter, and study real-world design patterns from the best reference sources.

---

## 1. Mobbin — The Gold Standard for Real-App Patterns

**What it is:** A searchable library of screenshots from real iOS, Android, and web apps, organized by screen type, flow, and UI element.

**How to use it effectively:**
- **Search by screen type:** "Login", "Onboarding", "Settings", "Empty state"
- **Search by flow:** "Checkout flow", "Signup flow", "Onboarding flow"
- **Search by element:** "Bottom sheet", "Modal", "Search bar"
- **Filter by platform:** iOS, Android, or Web
- **Filter by industry:** Finance, Health, Social, E-commerce, Productivity
- **Filter by app:** Search specific apps (Spotify, Airbnb, Stripe) to study their full pattern library

**What to look for when studying screens on Mobbin:**
1. Layout structure — Where is the primary content? Where are actions?
2. Component choices — What type of navigation, what input types, what feedback mechanisms?
3. Information hierarchy — What's largest? What's first? What has the most contrast?
4. Edge states — Do they show empty states? Error states? Loading states?
5. Consistency — How does this screen relate to other screens from the same app?

**Pro tips:**
- Save collections by project: "E-commerce checkout references", "SaaS onboarding examples"
- Compare 3-5 apps for the same screen type to identify the common pattern vs. unique innovations
- Pay attention to what's NOT on the screen — restraint is design skill
- Use the "Flows" section to see multi-screen sequences, not just individual screens

**URL:** mobbin.com

## 2. Screenlane — Newsletter-Style Inspiration by Screen Type

**What it is:** A curated library of UI screenshots organized by screen type and pattern, with regular additions.

**How to use it effectively:**
- **Browse by category:** Authentication, Onboarding, Dashboard, Profile, Settings, etc.
- **Filter by platform:** iOS or Android
- **Use as a checklist:** Before designing a screen type, browse Screenlane's category to see what others include

**What to look for:**
- How different apps solve the same screen type differently
- Which elements are universal vs. app-specific
- Typography and spacing patterns (Screenlane captures full-resolution screenshots)

**Best for:** Quick inspiration when starting a new screen type, seeing the full spectrum of approaches.

**URL:** screenlane.com

---

## 3. Refero — Real App Screenshots Organized by Pattern

**What it is:** A reference library of web design screenshots organized by page type, section type, and UI pattern.

**How to use it effectively:**
- **Browse by page type:** Landing, Pricing, About, Blog, Dashboard, Login
- **Browse by section:** Hero, Features, Testimonials, Footer, CTA
- **Filter by style:** Minimal, Bold, Dark, Colorful
- **Filter by industry:** SaaS, Agency, E-commerce, Portfolio

**What to look for:**
- Visual design treatment of specific sections (hero layouts, pricing table designs)
- How companies in your industry present similar information
- Responsive patterns (compare desktop captures across sites)

**Best for:** Web-specific inspiration, landing pages, marketing sites, visual treatment ideas.

**URL:** refero.design

---

## 4. Nicelydone — Curated by Flow Type

**What it is:** A collection of real product screens organized by user flow and screen type, focused on web products.

**How to use it effectively:**
- **Browse by flow:** Onboarding, Pricing, Login, Signup, Settings, Empty states
- **Study complete flows:** See how products handle multi-step processes end-to-end
- **Compare within category:** Multiple products' approaches to the same flow

**What to look for:**
- Flow sequence — How many steps? What order?
- Transition points — How do users move between steps?
- Copy and tone — How do products communicate during flows?

**Best for:** Understanding flow patterns (not just individual screens), web SaaS products.

**URL:** nicelydone.club

## 5. Dribbble & Behance — When to Use and When NOT to Use

**When to use:**
- Visual exploration: color palettes, typography pairings, illustration styles
- Animation and motion references (Dribbble supports video/GIF)
- Early concepting: getting unstuck with creative direction
- Specific UI element styling (button styles, card designs, icon sets)

**When NOT to use:**
- As functional design references (many shots are not from real products)
- For flow patterns (most shots are isolated screens, not connected flows)
- For accessibility references (many popular shots fail basic contrast/sizing)
- For mobile patterns (many shots are unrealistic screen dimensions)

**How to filter effectively on Dribbble:**
- Use tags: "mobile app", "dashboard UI", "checkout"
- Filter by "Made with Figma" for higher fidelity
- Sort by "Recent" not just "Popular" (popular skews toward flashy over functional)
- Look at the designer's other shots — are they from real products?

**Red flags to watch for:**
- Unrealistic data (clean numbers, perfect images, no edge cases)
- Tiny text below minimum readable size (looks good, not usable)
- No empty states, error states, or loading states shown
- Fantasy projects with no real constraints

**URLs:** dribbble.com, behance.net

## 8. How to Study a Reference — The 10-Point Checklist

When you find a screen or flow to reference, study it systematically. Do not just screenshot it and copy the layout. Ask these questions:

### Layout & Structure
1. **Grid system** — What grid is this using? How many columns? What are the margins and gutters? Where are the breakpoints?
2. **Visual hierarchy** — What do you see first? Second? Third? What's the intended reading flow (F-pattern, Z-pattern, focal point)?
3. **Spacing system** — What's the base unit? Is spacing consistent (4px/8px grid)? How much whitespace exists between sections?

### Components & Patterns
4. **Component inventory** — List every component on this screen. Which are standard platform components? Which are custom?
5. **Typography scale** — How many type sizes are used? What's the heading hierarchy? Is the body text readable (16px+ on web)?
6. **Color usage** — How many colors are used? What's the primary brand color? How is color used functionally (error=red, success=green, interactive=blue)?

### States & Edge Cases
7. **State coverage** — Does this reference show loading, empty, error, and populated states? If not, how would they handle those?
8. **Responsive behavior** — How does this adapt to different screen sizes? What happens on mobile vs. desktop?

### Interaction & Accessibility
9. **Interactive elements** — What's clickable/tappable? Are touch targets large enough? Are hover states shown?
10. **Accessibility** — Does this meet contrast requirements? Are labels visible? Would this work with a screen reader? Does it respect motion preferences?

### The Meta Question
After studying a reference, ask: **"What is this design optimizing for?"** Is it optimizing for speed (minimal steps), delight (beautiful interactions), trust (professional, secure feeling), or simplicity (reduced cognitive load)? Understanding the design intent helps you decide what to adopt and what to adapt for your specific product and users.

## 9. Building Your Own Reference Library

**Recommended workflow:**
1. Create folders by project or screen type (physical folders in Figma, bookmarks, or a tool like Eagle or Raindrop)
2. When you find a great reference, save it with context: app name, date, why you saved it
3. Before designing any screen, spend 15 minutes reviewing references for that screen type
4. Keep a "Hall of Fame" collection of the best patterns you've ever seen — revisit quarterly
5. Note patterns that appear across 3+ apps — those are conventions worth following
6. Note patterns that are unique to one app — evaluate if they are innovation or deviation

**Reference quantity guidelines:**
- For a screen type you've designed before: 3-5 references to check your assumptions
- For a new screen type: 8-12 references to understand the full spectrum of approaches
- For a complex flow: 5-8 complete flow references from real products
- For visual design direction: 15-20 mood references across sites, apps, and even physical design

**The 3-reference rule:**
Always study at least 3 different implementations before designing. One reference is copying. Two references is coincidence. Three references reveal the pattern.
