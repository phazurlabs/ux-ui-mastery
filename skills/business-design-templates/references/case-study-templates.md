# Case Study Templates — Portfolio and Client-Facing Project Narratives

## Case Study Narrative Structure

Every case study follows the same fundamental arc regardless of project type: establish the challenge, show the process, reveal the solution, and prove the results. The difference between a forgettable case study and a compelling one is specificity. Vague case studies read like marketing copy. Specific case studies read like evidence.

### The Four-Act Structure

**Act 1: Challenge (15-20% of the case study)**
Set the stage. Who is the client? What is their business? What problem were they facing? What were the stakes? Use specific numbers: revenue at risk, user complaints, competitive pressure, operational inefficiency. The reader should understand why this project mattered before they see a single design artifact.

**Act 2: Process (30-40% of the case study)**
This is where credibility is built. Show the research methods, the insights discovered, the ideation process, the design explorations that were rejected and why, the testing that validated or invalidated assumptions. This section differentiates a thoughtful designer from a pixel-pusher.

**Act 3: Solution (25-30% of the case study)**
The final design, presented in context. Not just screens in a mockup device — annotated designs that explain the thinking behind every significant decision. Why this layout? Why this hierarchy? Why this interaction pattern? Connect every design choice back to a research insight or business requirement from Act 1.

**Act 4: Results (15-20% of the case study)**
Prove it worked. Quantitative metrics wherever possible. Qualitative feedback (user quotes, client testimonials) to complement the numbers. Honest assessment of what worked well and what you would do differently. Lessons learned demonstrate professional maturity.

### Visual Storytelling Principles

**Show the artifact in context.** A login screen in a browser window on a desk with natural lighting tells a different story than a flat screenshot. Use device mockups, environment shots, and multi-screen compositions to convey the experience, not just the interface.

**Show progression.** Side-by-side comparisons of early sketches, wireframes, and final designs demonstrate the evolution of thinking. Annotate the transitions: "We moved the CTA above the fold after usability testing showed that 68% of users never scrolled past the hero section."

**Use data visualization.** Before/after metric comparisons should be visual, not just textual. A bar chart showing task completion time dropping from 4.2 minutes to 1.8 minutes is more impactful than a sentence stating the same fact.

**Annotate key decisions.** Callout boxes or numbered annotations on design screenshots draw attention to specific design decisions and their rationale. The reader should be able to scan the visuals and understand the story without reading every paragraph.

## Case Studies for Different Audiences

### For Potential Clients

**Emphasis:** Business outcomes, process reliability, communication quality, and ROI. Clients want to see that you understand business problems, not just design problems. Lead with the business challenge and the business results. Process should demonstrate professionalism and predictability.

**Tone:** Professional, consultative, business-focused. Minimize jargon. Frame design decisions in terms of user behavior and business metrics.

**Include:** Client testimonial, timeline, team structure, specific ROI numbers.

**Exclude:** Excessive design theory, tools-focused content, technical implementation details.

### For Hiring Managers

**Emphasis:** Your specific contribution, your thinking process, your growth. Hiring managers want to understand how you think, what decisions you made personally, and what your craft strengths are.

**Tone:** First-person, reflective, technically detailed. Use design vocabulary appropriate to the role level you are targeting.

**Include:** Your role clearly defined (especially on team projects), design explorations you personally drove, decisions you made and why, honest assessment of what you would do differently, specific tools and methods you employed.

**Exclude:** Vague team language that obscures your contribution ("we designed" without clarifying your role), portfolio pieces where you cannot articulate your specific decisions.

### For Design Community / Conference Talks

**Emphasis:** Novel insights, transferable frameworks, and lessons that benefit other designers. Community case studies are teaching tools.

**Tone:** Educational, generous with methodology, focused on the generalizable lesson rather than the specific client.

**Include:** Detailed methodology, frameworks that others can apply, honest failures and what they taught, tools and resources used.

**Exclude:** Client-confidential information (anonymize if needed), self-promotion that overshadows the educational content.
